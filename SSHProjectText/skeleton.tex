\begin{abstract} 
Network operators use visualizations to detect attacks on their systems, exploring and interacting with data to uncover patterns 
that would otherwise stay hidden. Operators then use these findings to block attackers and to expand the knowledge base of known threats. Detecting
anomalies and uncovering deeper patterns iteratively in data is at best, tedious and at worst, not achievable without data visualizations.
Previous works in network visualization analyzed the IP and AS dimensions of SSH logins without much focus on the username aspect of 
the attackers' attempts, detected anomalous network events only where baseline events were available, and enabled operators to share findings with one 
another. Little attention has been paid to whether attackers observed at different times represent the same persistent actor.
We present an iterative visualization SSHVisual, using login attempts collected on our own SSH-facing systems. SSHVisual analyzes the IP, AS, and username dimensions 
of SSH attacks, in service of SSH campaign detection. It complements automated detection by allowing operators to find anomalous events without normal baseline events. 
It correlates SSH attacks with real-world events, and it introduces a similarity metric that quantifies attacker persistence over time. Like previous 
systems, SSHVisual supports iterative and collaborative sharing of findings between operators. We evaluated SSHVisual using case studies and found that it helps us block attacks
and uncover patterns. We identified campaigns in which a set of attempted usernames recurred across unrelated IPs and ASes. 
The similarity metric surfaced attackers returning across different time periods despite changing network origin. SSHVisual shows that treating 
usernames as a first-class analytic dimension reveals attacker coordination that IP-centric analysis misses, and that exploratory, operator-driven 
detection remains valuable.

\end{abstract}

% =====================  I. INTRODUCTION  =====================
\section{Introduction}
Computer systems worldwide employ SSH \cite{ylonen1996ssh} to allow their users to securely connect to them in order to execute commands and do remote 
administration. A lot of users do a username, password combination (password based authentication) in order to gain entry to the SSH system. Attackers, 
who are adversarial in nature, can use brute force attack mechanisms \cite{295683} to get into SSH systems. Sometimes, they guess what the username, 
password combinations are and other times, they take advantage of poorly secured systems to build botnets \cite{295683} to carry out further attacks 
\cite{antonakakis2017understanding, kolias2017ddos} or steal resources from the systems, like cryptomining \cite{dang2019understanding, pastrana2019first}.

Given the prevalence of SSH attacks in the IPv4 space \cite{295683}, we collect SSH login data on our own systems and present SSHVisual. SSHVisual serves 
two kinds of network operators. Network administrators, who handle day-to-day SSH traffic, need to identify attackers quickly and block them. Security 
analysts, who study traffic over longer time periods, look for patterns that are not immediately apparent and share what they find across organizations. Consider 
a single time period: an administrator can isolate the highest-volume attacker and block it immediately, while an analyst examining the same window can iteratively
surface a set of low-frequency attackers that attempt an identical sequence of usernames, and group them as a coordinated botnet.

Surveys of network visualization \cite{7166305, 6007132, zhang2017survey, raynor2022state, wu2018research} establish the maturity of the field and the gaps 
we target. Our contributions follow from treating the username as an attacker identity. First, actor-level analysis: we treat the attacker as a 
first-class actor — including the username identity, not only the source IP or autonomous system (AS) — and we identify coordinated campaigns. Since network data 
affords no nromal baseline for automated detection \cite{gates2006challenging}, this identification is necessarily exploratory (therefore, not antagonistic but complementary to automated
detection with nromal baselines) — operators recognize coordination visually rather than specifying it in advance. Second, a similarity metric that 
quantifies attacker similarity and lets operators track how actors evolve in the username dimension across time periods. 
Third, correlation of actor behavior with real-world events, which is more tractable through usernames than IPs, given the limitations of IP corroboration.
Additionally, following established practices, we allow for iterative operator driven collaboration and labeling of irregularities found in the data.

The rest of the paper is as follows: Section II is Related Work, Section III is Tasks and Requirements, Section IV is Design, Section V is Evaluation and 
Section VI is Conclusion and Future Work.

% =====================  II. RELATED WORK  =====================
\section{Related Work}

A long line of tools \cite{mansmann2006interactive, oberheide2006flamingo, mansmann2009visual, taylor2009flovis, chen2014oceans, tremel2022vitalflow, xiao2006enhancing, goodall2009viassist} render network traffic and security events navigable for operators and analysts with the majority being network flow 
and traffic oriented. Additionally, a smaller alert- and log-oriented group \cite{livnat2005visual, king2014coordinated, jeong2023multi, jeong2022interactive, ceneda2016routingwatch,
roberts2007state, yi2008understanding, choi2009fast, humphries2013elvis, yu2010ember}
organizes IDS output and heterogeneous logs of which
\cite{jeong2023multi} contributes a visual analysis (of the CICIDS2017 dataset) that isolates attack clusters — including SSH brute-force (SSH-Patator) — through clustering and 
dimensionality reduction, namely a multi-resolution wavelet-feature analysis.


\subsection{Attack Campaigns with Time}

Honeypot literature does not promote the username identity as an actor in its own right (for attack campaign detection purposes): it remains an attribute to count, never a 
unit to track — the unit that, alongside the IP and AS, anchors our analysis. Some papers \cite{djap2021xb, visoottiviseth2011distributed, baykara2018novel, belqruch2019scada,
koniaris2013analysis, kyriakou2018container, koniaris2014honeypots} collect and chart honeypot traffic, with several of them 
capturing SSH credentials (usernames and passwords). Where credentials are charted — the SCADA SSH honeypot \cite{belqruch2019scada}, kippo-graph \cite{koniaris2013analysis}, 
and the container deployment \cite{kyriakou2018container} — 
usernames and passwords  appear only as static top-N tallies (kippo-graph's top-10  username and password bars are the archetype), never placed on a time axis even where 
the same tool charts attack volume over time.The WAN honeypot framework \cite{fraunholz2017distributed} is the closest prior system on the credential axis: it computes an IP-to-IP dictionary-overlap 
(containment) score to probe whether six same-/24 source IPs share a wordlist — a manual, one-off prototype of credential-based coordination analysis, performed on the 
IP unit and without a temporal dimension. 


\subsection{Attacker Similarity with Time}
Computed similarity between entities is well established in literature \cite{goodall2018situ, liao2012intelligent, pike2008putting, hao2015ensemble}. We do not claim similarity computation itself as novel; the contribution lies in the entity 
it operates on and how it moves through time. On the entity: these systems cluster internal hosts \cite{goodall2018situ}, behavioral profiles \cite{pike2008putting}, IP/port members \cite{hao2015ensemble}, or  internal enterprise users 
grouped by shared resources — not external attacker identities \cite{liao2012intelligent}. None treats the username as an attacker actor. On time: \cite{pike2008putting, hao2015ensemble} reach into the temporal dimension, 
but both do not compute the similarity of the attacker population across consecutive windows. We apply similarity based metrics — for example, Jaccard overlap of 
attackers between successive windows — particularly to the username actor unit, capturing how the attacker population persists or churns over time.

\subsection{Limits of automated detection}
One premise of our approach is that SSH honeypot traffic does not admit the normal baseline that automated anomaly and campaign detection presuppose. 
The broader literature already documents the fragility of such detection: Sommer and Paxson \cite{sommer2010outside} note that machine-learning anomaly detection 
is rarely deployed for intrusion detection given the variability of "normal" the cost of errors, and the difficulty of sound evaluation, 
while Axelsson \cite{axelsson2000base} shows it is throttled by the base-rate fallacy, where the false-positive rate becomes the limiting factor once 
intrusions are rare relative to benign traffic. Our setting departs from that one entirely, in the direction Gates and Taylor \cite{gates2006challenging} 
anticipate when they question whether attacks are rare or a stable normal exists, note that normal traffic may itself be malicious and malicious traffic 
unremarkable, and observe that detection ultimately devolves to an operator investigating clusters of activity by hand. The consequence is visible in the 
systems cited above: Situ \cite{goodall2018situ} learns a probabilistic model of each IP's behavior and CLIQUE \cite{best2011atypical} scores each actor 
against a baseline of its own expected activity, so both presume a per-actor "normal" that near-uniformly hostile, constantly churning traffic cannot supply. 
Situ nonetheless concedes that automation alone is insufficient — operators "do not want another automated tool with algorithms they do not trust" — and 
builds a human-in-the-loop system; we reach the same conclusion from a starker starting point, lacking even the per-actor baseline it assumes, and so 
treat campaign identification as operator-driven from the outset.

\subsection{Actor identity and real-world event correlation}
The attacks we observe do not arrive at a steady rate; they rise, fall, and change in character as events unfold in the wider world, so understanding 
them means lining them up against what is happening outside the network. The link is well documented: Bilge and Dumitras \cite{bilge2012before} found that the number of 
attacks against a given vulnerability can jump by up to five orders of magnitude — a hundred-thousand-fold increase — once it is publicly disclosed, and 
Sabottke et al. \cite{sabottke2015vulnerability} showed that future exploitation can be predicted from signals in disclosure channels alone. Allodi and Massacci \cite{allodi2012preliminary} add that a vulnerability's 
official severity score is a poor guide to whether it is actually attacked, so the dependable way to learn what attackers are reacting to is to watch real 
activity and line it up against real events, not to read it off a score. We want to do this at the level of a campaign — a group of attackers acting together — 
rather than one attacker at a time, and that raises a practical problem: a campaign rarely shows up as a single source address. IP addresses do not stay tied to 
one machine — one host is assigned many addresses over time \cite{xie2007dynamic}, hosts cycle through new addresses \cite{stone2009your}, and counting by address can overstate the number of 
real machines by orders of magnitude \cite{moura2015dynamic} — so it is likely that a view organized by address scatters one campaign across dozens of entries that look unrelated. 
The username pulls those pieces back together: the same login names likely recur across the changing addresses, so grouping by username re-assembles the 
campaign that an address-by-address view splits apart. A username is not a perfect identity either — names like "root" are tried by almost everyone, and 
one campaign runs through a whole list of names — so we treat it as one analysis dimension alongside the address and the network it belongs to (its AS), 
resolving the overlaps with the similarity and grouping methods above. 

\subsection{Operator labeling and collaboration}
Since detection on our data is operator-driven, the findings an operator produces by hand must be captured and shared rather than discarded. 
In two lines of work \cite{guerra2019study, xiao2006enhancing}, interactive labeling has been used to fold the operator's judgments back into the system. Collaborative analysis has been 
supported through shared artifacts with \cite{chen2014oceans} and \cite{goodall2009viassist} being notable examples. These systems establish that labeling and collaboration are valuable, 
which is why our labeling-and-sharing mechanism is the step that turns an identified set of usernames into a durable, shared campaign.


% ============  III. TYPICAL TASKS AND REQUIREMENTS  ============
\section{Tasks and Requirements}

The design of SSHVisual draws on the authors' experience as network operators and on gaps we identified in prior work on SSH attack analysis and security visualization. 
From these we derive three tasks and six requirements. Table~\ref{tab:task-req} summarizes which requirements support which tasks.

\subsection{Tasks}
\label{sec:tasks}

\textbf{T1: Identify SSH campaigns.} Security analysts need to find groups of attackers that behave alike across usernames, IP addresses, autonomous systems (ASes), and countries, 
and to record those groups so they can be referenced later.

\textbf{T2: Detect anomalies that warrant action.} Network administrators need to notice unusual activity, such as a sudden spike or a single source dominating traffic, 
quickly enough to decide whether to block it.

\textbf{T3: Share findings across operators.} Analysts and administrators on the same network need to see what their colleagues have already investigated, so that the 
same patterns are not identified twice.

\subsection{Requirements}
\label{sec:reqs}


\textbf{R1: Attacker similarity profiling.} The system should be able to algorithmically cluster similar attackers based primarily along the username 
dimension, secondarily along IP and AS information and tertiarily along other dimensions. R1 mostly serves T1 as it allows analysts to build a repository of similar attackers.

\textbf{R2: Sorting and ranking} SSH attack data is heavy-tailed: a few usernames~\cite{owens2008study} and IP addresses~\cite{wu2020mining}  account for most attempts, 
and the remaining entities are buried beneath them. The system should therefore list every entity in each dimension, not only the most active ones, 
and let the operator sort and rank entities by any combination of attributes. R2 supports T1 and T2: administrators can surface anomalous entities that never reach the 
top of a volume ranking, and analysts can find entities with similar attribute values.

\textbf{R3: Visual clarity.} Line charts become harder to read accurately as more series are added~\cite{javed2010graphical}. Each linechart should therefore show at most 
ten entities. An aggregate chart of total attacks should accompany the per-entity charts, so that operators can judge how much of the total any one entity accounts for. 
For example, if 150M attacks occur on a given day and 75M of them come from a single username, that username should stand out when the two charts are compared. R3 primarily supports T2.


\textbf{R4: Shared tagging.} Operators should be able to tag attackers, record both what a tag means and why it was applied to a particular attacker, 
and have those tags persist and remain visible to every operator using the system. Each tag application should record who applied it and when, 
so that later operators can judge whether a finding is still current. R4 primarily supports T3, and it supports T1 because tags accumulate into a repository of related attackers.


\textbf{R5: Complementary tabular and temporal views.} No single visualization serves every need. A table supports precise lookup and ranking but discards temporal structure; 
a linechart preserves temporal structure, showing spikes and campaigns at a glance, but conveys values less precisely. The system should provide both views and let operators 
move between them. An administrator may find and act on an anomaly using the table alone, whereas an analyst may use the table to assemble a set of related attackers and 
then examine their behavior over time in the linecharts. R5 supports T1 and T2.

 
\textbf{R6: Interactive exploration of the linecharts.} The linecharts should follow Shneiderman's Visual Information-Seeking Mantra: overview first, zoom and 
filter, then details-on-demand~\cite{shneiderman1996eyes}. They should open with an overview of the full time range, let the operator zoom into a time window 
and filter which entities are displayed, and reveal details about an individual entity on request. R6 supports T1 and T2: zooming isolates the time window of a 
suspected campaign, and filtering removes dominant entities that would otherwise mask smaller ones.

% =====================  IV. APPROACH  =====================
\section{Design}
\label{sec:design}
 
SSHVisual combines two visual components, a discovery table and a set of linecharts, with two non-visual components, similarity search and shared tagging. 
We describe the visual design in Section~\ref{sec:visual} and the non-visual design in Section~\ref{sec:nonvisual}. All figures show SSH login attempts 
collected between 1~November 2022 and 8~January 2023, a period of 69 days.

\subsection{Visual Design}
\label{sec:visual}

\subsubsection{Discovery Table}
\label{sec:table}

\begin{figure*}[!tb]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{Discovery.png}
  \caption{The discovery table, showing the countries tab. Each row summarizes one entity over the selected period, and operators can sort by any combination of columns or select up to ten rows to plot in the linecharts.}
  \label{fig:discovery}
\end{figure*}

The interface provides two views of the data. The discovery table (Figure~\ref{fig:discovery}) is independent of time: each row summarizes one entity over the selected period. 
The linecharts (Figure~\ref{fig:linecharts}) can be time-dependent. Both views cover the full data period by default.
 
The discovery table has four tabs, one per dimension: countries, IP addresses, ASN organizations, and usernames. In Figure~\ref{fig:discovery} the countries 
tab is active. Most columns are shared across tabs: total attacks, average daily attacks, persistence (the share of days on which the entity was active), 
the largest absolute and percentage day-over-day change, attacks in the most recent seven days, first- and last-seen dates, and the maximum daily count. 
Some columns apply only to particular tabs; for instance, the countries tab offers a Unique ASNs column that the ASN tab lacks. Operators choose which columns 
to display through the Columns menu. Each entity also carries a badge summarizing its dominant behavior, namely Scan, Spray, Botnet or Brute \textbf{[describe how badges are assigned]}. 
A search box filters rows by name, and the counter above the table reports the number of entities in the current tab (197 countries in Figure~\ref{fig:discovery}).

Unlike the linecharts, which show only the ten most active entities per dimension, the table lists every entity. Operators can select up to ten rows with the checkboxes 
and use Analyze Selected to plot those entities in the linecharts, and they can return to the linecharts at any time. This link lets operators move from a precise, 
ranked lookup to the temporal behavior of the same entities, fulfilling R5.

The linechart view (Figure~\ref{fig:linecharts}) stacks five charts on a shared daily time axis. The first, Total Attacks Over Time, shows the aggregate volume of 
attempts; the remaining four show the ten most active countries, ASN organizations, IP addresses, and usernames. Each legend entry lists the entity's total for the selected period.
 
Interaction with the linecharts follows Shneiderman's mantra~\cite{shneiderman1996eyes} (R6), and the charts are coordinated so that an action in any one of them updates all of them.
 
\textbf{Overview first.} On opening, the charts cover all 69 days of data, with each per-entity chart showing the ten most active entities in its dimension.
 
\textbf{Zoom.} Every chart supports brushing. When the operator brushes a time range on any chart, the range is applied to all five charts and recorded in the Active Filters bar, 
so the temporal context stays the same whichever dimension the operator is examining. Brushing is disabled once the range is two days or fewer, since a line needs more 
points to show a trend.
 
\textbf{Filter.} Left-clicking a legend entry selects that entity and filters every chart to the attacks associated with it. Because each attack attempt links a username, 
an IP address, an AS, and a country, the selection propagates along these relationships:

\begin{itemize}
  \item Selecting a country restricts the AS, IP, and username charts to the entities observed in attacks from that country.
  \item Selecting an AS shows the country it originates from, along with its IP addresses and the usernames they try.
  \item Selecting an IP address shows its country and AS, along with the usernames it tries.
  \item Selecting a username shows the countries, ASes, and IP addresses from which it was tried.
\end{itemize}
Right-clicking a legend entry crosses the entity out instead, removing it from its chart and rescaling the y-axis so that smaller series are no longer flattened against the 
axis by a dominant one.
 
\textbf{Details-on-demand.} Hovering over a data point shows a tooltip with that entity's attack count for the day, hovering over a legend entry highlights the entity's line, 
and the legend reports each entity's total for the selected period.
 
Linked filtering turns the linecharts from four separate rankings into a means of tracing an entity across dimensions. An operator investigating the spike on 30~December, 
for example, can select \texttt{root} in the username chart; the IP, AS, and country charts then show which addresses, networks, and countries tried \texttt{root}, and 
brushing around 30~December narrows the view to the spike itself. Conversely, selecting an AS such as DigitalOcean reveals which usernames its hosts try, indicating 
whether traffic from a hosting provider reflects one campaign or many. Brushing on every chart, linked filtering, and on-demand details together fulfill R6.

 
The linecharts answer questions that the discovery table cannot, such as ``Do the countries in Figure~\ref{fig:discovery} share the same peaks and valleys?'' or ``Does one country 
begin attacking earlier than another?'' In Figure~\ref{fig:linecharts}, the aggregate chart shows a sharp spike on 30~December, and most of the top ten countries peak on the same 
day, indicating a coordinated, multi-country event rather than activity from a single source. The charts also reveal differences in timing. China's largest peak falls in late 
November, weeks before the United States' activity rises, and the IP chart shows individual addresses that each spike for a day or two and then fall silent. Neither pattern can 
be recovered from the table's per-entity totals. Together, Figures~\ref{fig:discovery} and~\ref{fig:linecharts} show how the two views complement each other, fulfilling R5.

\subsubsection{Visual Clarity}
\label{sec:clarity}
 

Figure~\ref{fig:clarity} shows the upper portion of the linechart view at full size. Each per-entity chart is limited to ten series, because the accuracy with which 
people read multi-series line charts declines as the number of series grows~\cite{javed2010graphical}. Each chart has its own vertical scale, so that entities 
are legible even in dimensions with lower totals.
 
The aggregate chart serves as a reference for the charts below it (R3). On 30~December the aggregate chart peaks at roughly 9.5M attempts, while the username chart 
in Figure~\ref{fig:linecharts} shows \texttt{root} at roughly 5M on the same day, so a single username accounts for about half of the spike. Comparisons across 
per-entity charts are similarly direct. The United States and DigitalOcean both rise sharply on 9~December and remain elevated for the rest of the period, and a 
spike from Singapore on 24~November coincides with a spike from Tencent in the ASN chart, consistent with attacks launched from cloud infrastructure hosted in Singapore. 
Because each chart shows at most ten lines, such correspondences can be spotted at a glance.

\subsubsection{Username Similarity}
\label{sec:usersim}


Similarity search lets operators start from one attacker and retrieve the attackers that behave most like it. For usernames, the unit of analysis is the population of 
sources that tries a given username. For each username we compute the breadth of that population (the number of distinct IP addresses, ASes, and countries that try it), 
its volume, its persistence, and how concentrated its traffic is on its single most active source. We do not reuse the seventeen-dimensional profile we apply to 
IP addresses (Section~\ref{sec:ipsim}), because a diagnostic analysis showed several of those features to be uninformative for usernames. Day-to-day stability is the 
clearest example: most IP addresses are active on many days, so their stability is well defined, but about half of the usernames appear on only a single day, where 
stability is meaningless. We cluster the username profiles with $k$-means, selecting $k=9$. Each 
cluster is labeled automatically from its centroid. To answer a query, the system retrieves the query's cluster and ranks the other members by Euclidean distance to the query, 
reported as a similarity score.
 
In Figure~\ref{fig:usersim}, an operator searches for the username \texttt{james}. The system places it in Cluster~3, labeled High-Volume Persistent Botnet-Scale, 
which contains 2,624 usernames; the cluster summary reports that a typical member receives about 31.8K attempts from roughly 4,000 IP addresses and is active on 72\% of days. 
The results table lists the 20 most similar usernames, each with its total attacks, top source country, top three source IPs, and a set of ``Why similar?'' labels, such as a 
shared lead source country or comparable volume, that explain the match.
 
Above the results, a shared infrastructure panel lists IP addresses that appear among the top sources of more than one result, and these addresses are highlighted in the 
table. In Figure~\ref{fig:usersim}, ten addresses are shared. Two of them, 152.89.196.123 and 152.89.196.220, lie in the same /24 network and are top sources for 
\texttt{manager}, \texttt{daniel}, \texttt{oscar}, \texttt{sftp}, \texttt{admin1}, and several other usernames. The panel turns behavioral resemblance into infrastructural 
evidence: usernames tried by the same hosts are likely drawn from the same credential list and belong to the same campaign.

\subsubsection{IP Address Similarity}
\label{sec:ipsim}
 
 
We characterize each IP address by a seventeen-dimensional behavioral profile drawn entirely from its observed activity during the data period. The features fall into four 
groups: volume (log-scaled totals, daily means, and peaks); temporal shape (the fraction of days on which the address was active, its burst intensity, its largest day-over-day 
swings, a fitted trend slope, its activity span, and the share of its traffic in the final week); targeting behavior (the number of distinct usernames it tries, how stable 
that set is from one day to the next, its rotation rate, and the share of attempts taken by its most-tried username); and two flags indicating whether the address belongs to 
a cloud provider and whether it originates from a major source country. We cluster these profiles with $k$-means, label each cluster 
from its centroid, and answer queries in the same way as for usernames.
 
In Figure~\ref{fig:ipsim}, an operator searches for 61.177.173.46, which falls in Cluster~5, labeled Low-Volume Persistent Exploratory, containing 4,405 addresses. 
The most similar results are 61.177.173.53, 61.177.173.36, 61.177.173.47, and 61.177.172.124, with similarity scores between 91.2\% and 80.4\%, all belonging to 
CHINANET-BACKBONE. No feature in the profile encodes an address's network prefix or AS, yet the nearest neighbors of the query are adjacent addresses on the same network. 
Behavioral features alone therefore recover infrastructure relationships, suggesting that these hosts are operated together.
 
Figures~\ref{fig:usersim} and~\ref{fig:ipsim} show how SSHVisual lets operators assemble sets of similar attackers along both the username and IP dimensions and see why 
those attackers were grouped, fulfilling R1.


\subsubsection{Shared Tagging}
\label{sec:tagging}
 
 
 
 
 
Figures~\ref{fig:tag1}--\ref{fig:tag4} show how an operator records a finding so that other operators can see work that has already been done. Tags are organized in a 
tag repository for each dimension. The repository displays the current operator's name, and every tag and tag application records its author.
 
In Figure~\ref{fig:tag1} the repository is empty. To create a tag, the operator gives it a name, describes what it means, and picks a color; the interface prompts 
for the description with the reminder that it is what saves the next operator time. In Figure~\ref{fig:tag2} the operator has created a tag, ``similarity to james,'' 
for the username cluster found in Figure~\ref{fig:usersim}. The repository now lists the tag with its description, its author, and two counts: applications in the current 
dimension and applications across all dimensions, both zero so far.
 
Tags are applied from the similarity results. In Figure~\ref{fig:tag3} the operator selects seven usernames (\texttt{abc}, \texttt{jason}, \texttt{manager}, 
\texttt{info}, \texttt{daniel}, \texttt{bin}, and \texttt{oscar}), chooses the new tag, and adds a note explaining this particular application: ``recurring IP addresses.'' 
The selection reflects the evidence from the shared infrastructure panel. All seven usernames have highlighted, shared IP addresses among their top sources, whereas 
\texttt{jack}, despite the highest similarity score, has none and is left untagged. In Figure~\ref{fig:tag4} the repository reports seven applications. Filtering the 
list by tag shows each tagged username alongside its note, the operator who applied it, and a timestamp, and any application can be removed with Untag. 
Tagged usernames also appear in the Tags column of subsequent similarity searches, so an operator who later retrieves the same usernames sees immediately that 
they have already been investigated.
 
Separating a tag's meaning from the rationale for each application lets a later operator understand both what a group represents and why each member 
belongs to it, without repeating the analysis. The recorded author and timestamp indicate whether a finding is recent. Figures~\ref{fig:tag1}--\ref{fig:tag4} 
thus show how tagging supports collaboration among operators, fulfilling R4.
